# Dark fantasy adventure design

Skelmorrow is a dark fantasy adventure world. Derek's direction, 2026-10-24:
the early map became a catalogue of medieval livelihoods instead of places a
player would want to explore. Peat cutting, rope making, net drying and flax
retting explain how people survive; that alone does not make them destinations.
Adding wolves, a corpse or a sinister adjective does not resolve the problem.

## Start with the discovery

Choose the fantasy destination before its chores or quest objectives. A broken
watchtower whose warning was meant for something underground, a spider nursery
inside a collapsed shrine, an opened burial chamber, a forbidden crossing, or a
ruin occupied by a creature all suggest something to investigate. Specify the
actual location and encounter rather than filling the map from a list of trades.
Ruins, caves, towers, crypts, monster nests and dangerous natural wonders should
provide the adventure vocabulary; use them where the terrain and pacing suit.
Do not give every small discovery a dungeon or put the same tomb at every stop.

Before reserving or dressing a place, answer in plain sentences:

- What attracts the player, and what particular question does it raise?
- What changes as the player investigates beyond that first view?
- What can the player discover, recover, confront or decide there?
- What is the payoff: a worthwhile sight, knowledge, treasure, an encounter,
  an interaction or access to another part of the adventure?
- Which physical feature and supported gameplay make those answers true?

Use the existing brief's read, landmark, linked features, unique thing and set
piece, and the POI design's premise, journey and elements to carry these answers.
These are design judgments, not an invitation to add another checklist of props.

## Believable life supports the fantasy

Preserve functional layout, appropriate assets and the established textures.
Rats in nets are fine. A hut, boat, cart, rope rack, working family or trade can
give a fantastic place history, scale and a relationship to the town. They need
not all become magical, hostile or abandoned. Town services and travel pauses
can remain ordinary; do not present every economic activity as an adventure POI.

An ancient creature's exposed grave can contain peat workings around it. Nets
can hold evidence of a wreck that nobody remembers. Ropes can belong to a ruined
binding rite rather than merely a production lane. These are different premises
with different physical requirements, not names to paste over an unchanged yard.
Show the particular discovery and deliver its payoff. Unseen lore cannot carry
the whole location.

Dark fantasy includes the fantastic as well as danger and loss. Bandit crime,
poverty, hard labour, animal attacks and a miserable population do not establish
that identity by themselves. Vary wonder, forbidden knowledge, monstrous ecology,
ancient power, ruin, ritual, human ambition and the uncanny. Allow mystery and
breathing room. Avoid making every place a cursed workplace or every quest a
funeral errand.

A good smugglers' cache, treasure trail or ordinary monster den can be enough.
The reference is D&D/WoW adventure, not compulsory supernatural spectacle at
every stop. The difference is a concrete opportunity to explore and discover,
not whether every enemy or object is magical.

## Names and quests follow the adventure

Before assigning a character, deity or named creature a name, use
[the name registry](../name-registry.md) to look up its identity and reserve the
chosen name. Keep aliases attached to the same identity, and check dungeon and
town names together in the owning game's shared namespace.

Name a place for its memorable feature, creature, event or mystery. A player
should be able to distinguish it from the next stop and remember why they went
there. Adding Black, Blood, Hollow, Cursed or Forsaken to a trade is insufficient.
Keep strong existing names and suitable enemies; novelty alone is not a reason
to replace them. Read the zone's names together and remove repetitive motifs.

Quest offers explain what is at stake and why the player would investigate.
Objectives state the real actions clearly. Discoveries and completion dialogue
pay off the particular question rather than congratulating the player for
fetching another commodity. A short rat hunt can be a good first encounter when
it opens or resolves a concrete discovery. Not every task needs an epic villain.
Use supported quest blocks, stable references and the place-token rules in
`docs/town-npcs.md`; prose cannot implement a ritual, choice or transformation.
Keep an essential clue, offer or completion payoff together in the text the
player actually receives. Dialogue text lists are rotating small talk, not
paragraphs displayed together; do not split a confession or its evidence across
separate visits by accident. Check the resolved text against the live NPC's
actual places table, not only the larger authoring lookup.

## Review before multiplying

Judge the first finished representative area at player height before repeating
it. Under rubric P1, identify the actual fantasy premise and its visible evidence;
under P4, state the attraction, investigation and payoff. A well arranged trade
yard with generic danger is returned at this stage. Numerical area, catalogue
reuse and working objectives remain necessary where applicable, but cannot pass
this design judgment.

For a narrative retrofit, keep live text honest about existing geography and
mechanics. Record required ruins, interiors, nests, encounters and interactions
as unbuilt work in the world's living authoring plan. Do not advertise an
enterable crypt that has no entrance, describe a proposed boss as spawned, or
call a renamed work yard a completed physical conversion. Old construction
reviews are historical evidence; they do not approve a newly rejected premise.
